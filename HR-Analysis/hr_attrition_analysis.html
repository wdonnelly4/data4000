Employee Attrition and Workload Analysis¶

Willem Donnelly

This notebook looks at two questions from our HR director:

  1. How do employees who left the company compare with employees who stayed?
  2. Do employees who work longer hours tend to receive higher performance evaluations?

The analysis uses the full HR_comma_sep.csv dataset, where each row is one employee record.

2. Compare Employees Who Left With Those Who Stayed¶

Chosen measure: employee satisfaction (satisfaction_level)

Satisfaction is scored from 0 (least satisfied) to 1 (most satisfied). It matters to HR because it's one of the few warning signs a company can measure before someone quits. If employees who left were noticeably less satisfied, regular satisfaction surveys could help HR spot people at risk of leaving while there's still time to act.

3. Test the Difference in Means¶

Null hypothesis (H₀): The average satisfaction level of employees who left is the same as the average satisfaction level of employees who stayed.

Alternative hypothesis (H₁): The average satisfaction level of employees who left is different from the average satisfaction level of employees who stayed.

I use Welch's t-test because it doesn't assume both groups have the same spread. That fits here, since the standard deviations are 0.264 for employees who left and 0.217 for those who stayed.

In [6]:
left_sat = df.loc[df["employment_status"] == "Left", "satisfaction_level"]
stayed_sat = df.loc[df["employment_status"] == "Stayed", "satisfaction_level"]

# Welch's two-sided t-test, Left group first
t_result = stats.ttest_ind(left_sat, stayed_sat, equal_var=False)
mean_difference = left_sat.mean() - stayed_sat.mean()

def format_p(p):
    # Very small p-values can underflow to 0.0 in floating point
    if p == 0:
        return "< 1e-300 (too small for the computer to represent exactly)"
    return f"{p:.2e}"

print(f"Mean satisfaction, Left:   {left_sat.mean():.4f}")
print(f"Mean satisfaction, Stayed: {stayed_sat.mean():.4f}")
print(f"Mean difference (Left - Stayed): {mean_difference:.4f}")
print(f"t statistic: {t_result.statistic:.2f}")
print(f"p-value: {format_p(t_result.pvalue)}")

Mean satisfaction, Stayed: 0.6668
Mean difference (Left - Stayed): -0.2267
t statistic: -46.64
p-value: < 1e-300 (too small for the computer to represent exactly)

Technical interpretation: The t statistic is about −46.64, and the p-value is far below 0.05 (smaller than 1 × 10⁻³⁰⁰). We reject the null hypothesis. There is a statistically significant difference in mean satisfaction between employees who left and employees who stayed.

What this means for HR: Employees who stayed had the higher average satisfaction, 0.667, compared with 0.440 for employees who left. That's a gap of about 0.23 on a 0-to-1 scale, the equivalent of 23 points on a 100-point survey. The gap is far too large to be explained by random chance. In short, people who left the company were, on average, a lot less satisfied than people who stayed.

Do employees who work longer hours tend to receive higher evaluations? Yes, somewhat. The correlation is r = 0.34, a positive but moderate-to-weak relationship. Employees who work more hours tend to have somewhat higher evaluations, and the scatterplot shows this as a gradual upward drift from left to right.

Statistical significance: The p-value is far below 0.05, so the relationship is statistically significant. With almost 15,000 employees, though, even a modest pattern will come out significant. Significance tells us the pattern is real, not how strong it is.

How useful hours are for one employee: Hours tell us very little about any one person's evaluation. Squaring the correlation (r² ≈ 0.12) shows that hours line up with only about 12% of the variation in evaluation scores, which leaves about 88% unexplained. The scatterplot makes this clear: at almost every hours level, evaluations range from the 0.4s to 1.0. Someone working 250 hours a month could easily have a lower score than someone working 150. The plot also shows a dense cluster of employees working about 130 to 160 hours with evaluations around 0.45 to 0.57, a group that could be worth a closer look on its own. Hours are one small piece of the picture, not a reliable way to predict how an individual will be evaluated.

5. HR Briefing¶

Employees who left the company were noticeably less satisfied than those who stayed, with an average satisfaction score of 0.44 compared with 0.67, a gap of about 23 points on a 100-point scale. Longer hours were only modestly connected to better reviews (correlation of 0.34), and hours account for just about 12% of the differences in evaluation scores, so working more hours is far from a guarantee of a strong review. Because this data describes what already happened rather than coming from a controlled experiment, it shows that low satisfaction and leaving go together, but it can't prove that low satisfaction caused people to leave. As a next step, HR could look into whether the employees who left with very low satisfaction were also the ones carrying the heaviest workloads, such as the most projects or the longest hours, to see whether burnout is driving attrition.
